Research

Weather Jiu-Jitsu

A framework for gently redirecting or deflecting extreme weather — hurricanes, atmospheric rivers, freezes, heatwaves — with scientifically informed, low-energy interventions in the atmosphere.

Inspired by martial arts philosophy, the approach partners with nature rather than resisting its force. Instead of large-scale geoengineering, it asks whether small, precisely targeted perturbations can nudge jet streams, vortices, and mesoscale eddies away from the pathways that lead to destructive extremes — reducing hazard severity and increasing lead time.

Steering extremes in AI weather foundation models

Using deep-learning weather models such as Aurora and Pangu, I run perturbation experiments that locate the sensitive spatiotemporal zones where small upstream changes have outsized downstream effects — then ask whether a targeted nudge there can move a storm.

For Hurricane Sandy (2012), two pathways emerge. In the Caribbean, forward finite-time Lyapunov exponent diagnostics locate dynamically sensitive regions inside Sandy’s steering flow, where targeted perturbations produce larger and more systematic track responses than random placement. In the Pacific, perturbations in a corridor near 165°W act remotely through a Rossby-wave teleconnection. Both produce only modest offsets over the first five days — then amplify sharply at recurvature, giving modelled track deviations of 360–580 km by day seven.

The same framework identifies where additional observations would most improve tropical-cyclone track forecasts.

Fire and compound hazards

Wildfires, floods, and landslides are often triggered by the same persistent circulation patterns and arrive in clusters that overwhelm local-scale risk management. I model how they interact across natural and human systems.

MauiFireScape, an agent-based model in NetLogo, couples wildfire spread with cascading power outages and evacuation behaviour to find the tipping points where a community’s response collapses. Related work covers flood and landslide exposure during the 2023 California atmospheric river event, and post-fire landslide susceptibility around Lake Oroville after the 2020 North Complex Fire.
