Research

Weather Jiu-Jitsu

Weather Jiu-Jitsu is a research framework that explores how we might gently redirect or deflect extreme weather — hurricanes, atmospheric rivers, freezes, and heatwaves — using scientifically informed, low-energy interventions in the atmosphere.

Inspired by martial arts philosophy, the approach aims to partner with nature rather than resist its force. Instead of large-scale geoengineering or brute-force methods, it investigates whether small, precisely targeted perturbations can nudge atmospheric circulations — jet streams, vortices, mesoscale eddies — away from the pathways that lead to destructive extremes, reducing hazard severity and increasing lead time.

Keywords: chaos control · nonlinear dynamics · Lorenz-63 / Lorenz-84 · adaptive perturbation

Steering extremes in AI weather foundation models

Using deep-learning weather models such as Aurora and Pangu, I run perturbation experiments that locate the sensitive spatiotemporal zones where small upstream changes have outsized downstream effects — then ask whether a targeted nudge there can move a storm.

For Hurricane Sandy (2012), two distinct pathways emerge. In the Caribbean, forward finite-time Lyapunov exponent diagnostics locate dynamically sensitive regions inside Sandy’s steering flow, where targeted perturbations produce larger and more systematic track responses than random placement. In the Pacific, perturbations in a preferred corridor near 165°W act on Sandy remotely through a Rossby-wave teleconnection, confirmed with Takaya–Nakamura wave-activity-flux diagnostics. Both pathways produce only modest offsets of a few tens of kilometres over the first five days — but those offsets are rapidly amplified once Sandy enters its highly sensitive recurvature region, yielding modelled track deviations of 360–580 km after seven days.

Beyond early intervention, the same framework identifies where additional observations would most improve tropical-cyclone track forecasts. Related work extends the approach to redirecting atmospheric rivers before landfall, and to benchmarking how faithfully these AI models reproduce extremes in the first place.

Keywords: Aurora · Pangu · tropical cyclones · atmospheric rivers · targeted perturbations · FTLE

Fire and compound hazards

Wildfires, floods, and landslides are often triggered by the same persistent circulation patterns and arrive in clusters that overwhelm local-scale risk management. I model these interactions across natural and human systems — from an agent-based model of wildfire spread, cascading power outages, and evacuation behavior (MauiFireScape, NetLogo), to remote-sensing assessment of flood and landslide exposure during the 2023 California atmospheric river event, to post-fire landslide susceptibility mapping around Lake Oroville after the 2020 North Complex Fire.

Keywords: agent-based modeling · cascading failure · remote sensing · Google Earth Engine
